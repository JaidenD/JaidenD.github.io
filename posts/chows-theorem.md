In this note we will first build up to Chow’s theorem and then discuss its implications in physics and control.

Let $\mathcal D$ be a $C^\infty$-distribution over a base manifold $M$. We wish to leave open the possibility of $\mathcal D$ having non-constant rank among points on $M$, hence we view $\mathcal D$ as a sheaf of vector fields rather than as a subbundle of $TM$. We use $\Gamma^\infty(\mathcal D)$ to denote smooth sections of $\mathcal D$.

In classical differential equation theory the central problem is to find integral curves of a vector field. The natural generalization of this idea is to find integral manifolds of distributions. We will quickly outline this idea and state a classical result. For simplicity, we will work purely with smooth objects.

A local integral manifold through $x_0$ of $\mathcal D$ is an immersed submanifold $S$ of a neighborhood $U$ of $x_0$ such that, for all $x \in S$,
$$
T_xS \subseteq \mathcal D_x.
$$
A local integral manifold is maximal if
$$
T_xS = \mathcal D_x
$$
for all $x \in S$. If a maximal local integral manifold for $\mathcal D$ containing $x$ contains all other maximal local integral manifolds through $x$, then it is said to be a maximal integral manifold. The distribution $\mathcal D$ is called integrable if every point has a maximal integral manifold through it.

We now introduce a seemingly unrelated idea: involutivity. The distribution $\mathcal D$ is said to be involutive if it is closed under the Lie bracket,
$$
[X,Y] = X(Y)-Y(X).
$$
That is, $\mathcal D$ forms a Lie algebra.

Surprisingly, these two ideas are beautifully related by the classical Frobenius theorem:

::: theorem Frobenius
$$
\mathcal D \text{ is integrable}
\iff
\mathcal D \text{ is involutive}
$$
:::

By Zorn’s lemma, one can also show that given a distribution $\mathcal D$, there exists a minimal involutive distribution containing $\mathcal D$, called the involutive closure of $\mathcal D$. We denote it by $\mathrm{Lie}^{(\infty)}(\mathcal D)$. Accepting this lemma without proof for now, we show how to construct $\mathrm{Lie}^{(\infty)}(\mathcal D)$.

Recursively define
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Lie}^{(l)}(\mathcal D)_x
&=
\mathrm{Lie}^{(l-1)}(\mathcal D)_x \\
&\quad
+
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb R}
\left\{
[X,Y](x)
:
X \in \Gamma^\infty\!\left(
\mathrm{Lie}^{(l_1)}(\mathcal D)
\right),
\right. \\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.
Y \in \Gamma^\infty\!\left(
\mathrm{Lie}^{(l_2)}(\mathcal D)
\right),
\;
l_1+l_2=l-1
\right\},
\\
\mathrm{Lie}^{(0)}(\mathcal D)_x
&=
\mathcal D_x.
\end{aligned}
$$

By the previously mentioned lemma, this recursion becomes stationary pointwise for finite $m(x)$:
$$
\mathrm{Lie}^{(n)}(\mathcal D)_x
=
\mathrm{Lie}^{(n+1)}(\mathcal D)_x,
\qquad
\forall n \geq m(x).
$$

Hence, as the algebraists would say, the involutive closure of $\mathcal D$ is obtained by taking the pointwise direct limit of $\mathrm{Lie}^{(l)}(\mathcal D)$:
$$
\mathrm{Lie}^{(\infty)}(\mathcal D)_x
=
\varinjlim_l \mathrm{Lie}^{(l)}(\mathcal D)_x.
$$

Switching gears for a moment, we can also use $\mathcal D$ to generate a set of diffeomorphisms on the base $M$. Let $\Phi_t^X$ be the time-$t$ flow generated by the vector field $X$. Define $\mathrm{Diff}(\mathcal D)$ to be the set of diffeomorphisms of $M$ of the form
$$
\Phi_{t_1}^{X_1}
\circ
\cdots
\circ
\Phi_{t_k}^{X_k},
\qquad
t_1,\dots,t_k \in \mathbb R,
\quad
X_1,\dots,X_k \in \Gamma^\infty(\mathcal D),
\quad
k \in \mathbb N.
$$
Note that this definition allows negative-time flows along a vector field.

Define the orbit of a point $x_0$ by
$$
\mathcal O(x_0,\mathcal D)
=
\left\{
\phi(x_0)
:
\phi \in \mathrm{Diff}(\mathcal D)
\right\}.
$$

In isolation, this object would be pretty horrible to compute, since one would have to compute the flows of $k$ vector fields. However, amazingly, we can describe the orbit set using $\mathrm{Lie}^{(\infty)}(\mathcal D)$.

::: theorem Orbit theorem
Let $\mathcal D$ be a smooth distribution on $M$, and let $x_0 \in M$. Then the following statements hold:

1. $\mathcal O(x_0,\mathcal D)$ is a smooth immersed submanifold.
2. For each $x \in \mathcal O(x_0,\mathcal D)$,
   $$
   \mathrm{Lie}^{(\infty)}(\mathcal D)_x
   \subseteq
   T_x \mathcal O(x_0,\mathcal D).
   $$
3. If $x \sim y$ means $y \in \mathcal O(x,\mathcal D)$, then
   $$
   M
   =
   \bigsqcup_{[x] \in M/{\sim}}
   \mathcal O(x,\mathcal D).
   $$
:::

Note that if the distribution were analytic rather than smooth, then the inclusion in point 2 would be an equality. To illustrate this, we show an example of a smooth distribution where this inclusion is strict.

Let
$$
f(x)
=
\begin{cases}
e^{-1/x^2}, & x \neq 0, \\
0, & x = 0.
\end{cases}
$$
You may remember from your analysis class that $f$ is smooth but not analytic.

Define a smooth distribution
$$
\mathcal D
=
\operatorname{span}\{X,Y\},
\qquad
X = \partial_x,
\qquad
Y = f(x)\partial_y.
$$

Since $Y(0,y)=0$, we have
$$
\mathcal D_{(0,y)}
=
\operatorname{span}\{\partial_x\}.
$$
Computing the first Lie bracket gives
$$
[X,Y]
=
f'(x)\partial_y.
$$

Acting by $X$ $k$ times via the Lie bracket gives $k$ nested Lie brackets, denoted $\mathrm{ad}_X^k(\cdot)$, so computing higher brackets gives
$$
\mathrm{ad}_X^k(Y)
=
f^{(k)}(x)\partial_y.
$$

In particular, $f$ is flat at zero, so
$$
f^{(k)}(0)=0
\qquad
\text{for all } k \geq 0.
$$
Therefore every iterated Lie bracket evaluated at $(0,y)$ gives no $\partial_y$-direction. Hence
$$
\mathrm{Lie}^{(\infty)}(\mathcal D)_{(0,y)}
=
\operatorname{span}\{\partial_x\}.
$$

However, the orbit through $(0,y)$ is two-dimensional. Start at $(0,y)$, then flow along $X$ to $(\varepsilon,y)$ with $\varepsilon \neq 0$. At this new point,
$$
Y(\varepsilon,y)
=
f(\varepsilon)\partial_y
\neq 0.
$$
Thus, one can move in the $y$-direction and then flow back along $X$ to $x=0$. The net action of this flow is a translation along the $y$-direction despite the Lie brackets at $x=0$ missing the direction.

Therefore,
$$
T_{(0,y)}
\mathcal O((0,y),\mathcal D)
=
T_{(0,y)}\mathbb R^2
=
\operatorname{span}\{\partial_x,\partial_y\},
$$
while
$$
\mathrm{Lie}^{(\infty)}(\mathcal D)_{(0,y)}
=
\operatorname{span}\{\partial_x\}.
$$
Thus we get the strict inclusion
$$
\mathrm{Lie}^{(\infty)}(\mathcal D)_x
\subset
T_x\mathcal O(x_0,\mathcal D).
$$

We now arrive at Chow’s theorem as a corollary of the orbit theorem.

::: theorem Chow
If $M$ is connected and if, for a $C^\infty$-distribution $\mathcal D$ on $M$,
$$
\mathrm{Lie}^{(\infty)}(\mathcal D)=TM,
$$
then
$$
\mathcal O(x_0,\mathcal D)=M.
$$
:::
